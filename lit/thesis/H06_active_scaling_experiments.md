# H6: Active Experiment Design for Scaling Laws and Hyperparameter Transfer

## 1. Hypothesis and SOFisher mapping

Each small-scale training run (a point in model size, data size, mixture, learning rate space) is an expensive measurement whose outcome is costly to obtain but whose value is predictable from cheap features (loss curves so far, neighboring runs, known scaling exponents) because the loss surface is smooth across these dimensions. A learned or principled policy that sequentially chooses the next run to launch, conditioning on all prior runs, should fit a scaling law or locate a hyperparameter optimum to a target accuracy using substantially fewer runs than grid or random sweeps, while also reporting calibrated extrapolation uncertainty for the large run it is ultimately meant to inform.

SOFisher mapping: field-of-view imaging choice maps to (model size, data size, mixture, LR) choice; spatial autocorrelation of rare targets maps to smoothness/monotonicity of the loss surface across scale and hyperparameters; the DQN's cheap state features (already-imaged neighbors) map to already-run configurations and their partial loss curves; the expensive measurement (full imaging pass) maps to a full or partial training run, which costs orders of magnitude more than the "state" used to decide it.

## 2. Prior art

- Kaplan et al. 2020, "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models," arXiv:2001.08361. Power-law loss scaling with model size, data, and compute across 7+ orders of magnitude; motivates compute-allocation formulas later revised by Chinchilla.
- Hoffmann et al. 2022, "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (Chinchilla), arXiv:2203.15556. ~400 models, 70M-16B params, 5-500B tokens; found model size and data should scale equally; 70B Chinchilla beats 280B Gopher and 175B GPT-3 on MMLU (67.5% avg).
- Yang et al. 2022, "Tensor Programs V: Tuning Large Neural Networks via Zero-Shot Hyperparameter Transfer" (muP/muTransfer), arXiv:2203.03466. Tuning on a 40M-param proxy and zero-shot transferring beat published 6.7B GPT-3 numbers at ~7% of total pretraining tuning cost.
- Snoek, Larochelle, Adams 2012, "Practical Bayesian Optimization of Machine Learning Algorithms," NeurIPS 2012. GP-based Bayesian optimization for ML hyperparameters; beat prior best CIFAR-10 result (14.98% test error) with fewer trials than grid/random search.
- Li, Jamieson, DeSalvo, Rostamizadeh, Talwalkar 2017 (JMLR 18), "Hyperband: A Novel Bandit-Based Approach to Hyperparameter Optimization," arXiv:1603.06560. Adaptive resource allocation/early-stopping over random search; reports over an order-of-magnitude speedup versus competing methods on several deep learning tasks.
- Falkner, Klein, Hutter 2018, "BOHB: Robust and Efficient Hyperparameter Optimization at Scale," ICML 2018 / arXiv:1807.01774. Combines Bayesian optimization with Hyperband's budget allocation; reported to strongly outperform both plain Hyperband and plain BO baselines across benchmark suites (relative speedups, exact numbers per-benchmark, UNVERIFIED here).
- Li, Jamieson, Rostamizadeh, Gonina, Hardt, Recht, Talwalkar 2020, "A System for Massively Parallel Hyperparameter Tuning" (ASHA), arXiv:1810.05934. Asynchronous successive halving; scales linearly with workers, demonstrated at 500 workers, reported to outperform prior state-of-the-art HPO methods.
- Choshen, Zhang, Andreas 2024, "A Hitchhiker's Guide to Scaling Law Estimation," arXiv:2410.11840 (ICML 2025). 485 published model families, 1000+ fitted scaling laws; fitting on intermediate checkpoints (not just final loss) substantially improves extrapolation accuracy, and predictions are most accurate from models of similar size to the target.
- Porian, Wortsman, Jitsev, Schmidt, Carmon 2024, "Resolving Discrepancies in Compute-Optimal Scaling of Language Models," arXiv:2406.19146 (NeurIPS 2024). Reconciles Kaplan vs. Chinchilla via three confounds (last-layer FLOPs accounting, warmup duration, scale-dependent optimizer tuning); with corrections, Kaplan-style fits agree closely with Chinchilla's.
- Ye, Liu, Sun, Zhan, Zhou, Qiu 2024, "Data Mixing Laws: Optimizing Data Mixtures by Predicting Language Modeling Performance," arXiv:2403.16952. Fits validation loss as a sum-of-exponentials function of mixture ratios across domains to predict and select optimal mixtures without training the full mixture grid.
- Liu et al. 2024, "RegMix: Data Mixture as Regression for Language Model Pre-training," arXiv:2407.01492 (ICLR 2025). Trained 512 1M-param models on 1B tokens each, regressed to predict best mixture, then trained a 1B model on 25B tokens (1000x larger, 25x longer) that beat all 64 human/baseline mixture candidates; matched or beat DoReMi at 10% of its compute.
- Bergstra... / general: "Scaling Laws for Hyperparameter Optimization," arXiv:2302.00441. Frames HPO curves themselves as obeying scaling laws usable to guide multi-fidelity search; details of reported gains UNVERIFIED here (not fetched in full).
- Zhang, Andreas, et al. 2026, "Spend Less, Fit Better: Budget-Efficient Scaling Law Fitting via Active Experiment Selection" (SL²), arXiv:2604.22753. Directly poses scaling-law fitting as budget-aware sequential experimental design over a pool of heterogeneous-cost runs; uncertainty-aware active selection reaches within 1% of the target-region loss/optimum on LR and batch-size scaling for 1B-param LLMs using roughly 10% of the full sweep budget, and approaches full-sweep accuracy elsewhere. This is the closest existing instantiation of H6's core idea; UNVERIFIED whether it covers joint (model size, data size, mixture, LR) space or only 1-2 axes at a time.
- Adaptive Data Optimization (Jiang, Diao, et al.), "Adaptive Data Optimization: Dynamic Sample Selection with Scaling Laws," arXiv:2410.11820. Online, per-domain scaling laws used to adjust data mixture concurrently with a single training run rather than across many runs; related but a different operating point (within-run vs. across-run active design).

## 3. What is genuinely open as of 2026

SL² (arXiv:2604.22753, 2026) establishes that active, uncertainty-aware selection beats fixed grids for fitting a scaling law along one or two axes (LR, batch size) at a fixed model family and fixed data. What remains open, precisely: (a) joint active design across model size, data size, mixture, and LR simultaneously, where the policy must trade off which axis to perturb next, not just which point on a pre-chosen axis; (b) transfer of the learned policy itself across model families and architectures, i.e., does a policy trained to pick informative runs for one codebase/tokenizer/architecture generalize to a new one, analogous to muTransfer's zero-shot transfer of hyperparameters rather than of the search policy; (c) calibrated extrapolation uncertainty that is validated against held-out large runs at industrial compute scale, not just cross-validated within the small-scale pool, given that Porian et al. show naive fits can disagree by large margins due to confounds like optimizer tuning and warmup that a policy must implicitly account for; (d) whether an active policy remains beneficial once mixture (Ye et al., RegMix) and hyperparameter (muP) axes are combined with model/data scaling in one search space, since these literatures have so far stayed largely separate. A year is needed because validating (c) requires running a genuinely larger held-out model as ground truth, which is the expensive step the whole method exists to avoid, so the schedule must front-load cheap ladder experiments and only spend the large validation run once assumptions are checked.

## 4. Three-condition check

- Spatial (here: scale/hyperparameter-space) autocorrelation of the target: scaling laws are power laws in model size and data (Kaplan 2020, Hoffmann 2022) and mixture loss is a smooth sum-of-exponentials in mixture ratios (Ye et al. 2024), and muP shows optimal LR is stable/predictable across width (Yang et al. 2022) — all three axes exhibit strong, low-dimensional smoothness, satisfying autocorrelation.
- Expensive measurement vs. cheap state: a training run (GPU-hours, days) is vastly more expensive than reading off a partial loss curve or a neighboring run's fitted exponent; Choshen et al. 2024 explicitly show intermediate checkpoints (cheap, already-available signal) improve prediction accuracy, confirming a cheap-state/expensive-outcome asymmetry exists to exploit.
- Cheap features alone cannot rank configurations: Porian et al. 2024 show that naive extrapolation from small-scale fits (Kaplan-style) without correcting confounds gives substantially wrong compute-optimal predictions versus Chinchilla, and RegMix shows human/heuristic mixture selection is reliably beaten by learned regression — meaning static heuristics on cheap features are not sufficient and a fitted/learned model is required, satisfying the third condition.

## 5. Year plan

Phase 1 (weeks 1-6, 4 A100s): Build a small-scale training ladder (GPT-2-style decoder, 10M-300M params, 100M-3B tokens, a fixed 2-3 domain data mixture, LR as the free axis) reproducing a Chinchilla-style fit at this scale as calibration. Implement a simple active policy (Gaussian-process or ensemble-based uncertainty over a parametric scaling-law family, in the spirit of SL² arXiv:2604.22753) that picks the next (size, tokens, LR) triple to run. Success metric: number of runs needed for the fitted law to predict a held-out larger run (2-4x the largest training-set run) within a target error band (e.g. 10% loss, then tighten to 5%); strongest existing baseline to beat is SL²'s own reported ~10x reduction versus grid/random on a single-axis version of this problem, and classical Hyperband/BOHB/ASHA as the general multi-fidelity baseline.

Phase 2 (months 2-6): Extend the search space to jointly include data mixture (adding a RegMix/data-mixing-law style axis) and test whether the same active policy, or a policy retrained per-axis, still beats per-axis grids run independently. Begin testing policy transfer: train the policy on one architecture/tokenizer, evaluate whether it picks informative runs for a second architecture without retraining, mirroring muTransfer's zero-shot claim but for the search policy rather than the hyperparameters.

Phase 3 (months 6-12): Scale validation: use the fitted policy plus law to predict a genuinely larger held-out run (largest affordable on available hardware, ideally an order of magnitude above the training ladder) and report calibrated interval coverage, not just point accuracy. Compare total compute spent (ladder + policy overhead) against equivalent-accuracy grid/random/Hyperband baselines run to the same target error band, and write up guidance for how many runs a lab needs per axis added to the search space.

## 6. Kill criteria

- If, by end of Phase 1, the active policy needs more than half of a matched grid sweep's runs to hit the same extrapolation error (i.e. it barely beats naive grid search at this small scale), the core smoothness/cheap-state assumption is likely too weak at this granularity and the approach should stop before Phase 2.
- If the fitted scaling law's held-out prediction (Phase 3) is off by more than 2x the in-sample cross-validation error, extrapolation uncertainty is not calibrated and the method cannot be trusted for real budget decisions; stop and report as a negative result rather than proceeding to policy-transfer claims.
- If adding the mixture axis in Phase 2 causes the policy's run-count advantage over per-axis grids to collapse (interaction effects dominate, consistent with RegMix's finding that "domains interact in complex ways" beating simple additive assumptions), scope the contribution down to single/dual-axis active design and drop the joint-space claim rather than forcing it.

## 7. Impact

If it works, a lab replaces its current practice, an internally maintained or ad hoc grid/random sweep of small models followed by a Chinchilla-style curve fit before committing to a frontier run's size, data budget, and hyperparameters, with a sequential procedure: launch a small seed set of runs, let the policy pick each next run based on all prior results and current uncertainty, and stop once the predicted interval for the target large-run loss is tight enough to commit compute. Concretely this changes headcount-hours and GPU-hours spent on pre-training-law fitting (Choshen et al.'s 485-model dataset shows how large and ad hoc this practice already is across the field), and replaces fixed muP-style proxy-model tuning with an adaptively chosen proxy schedule, while giving a defensible, quantified uncertainty band to attach to the go/no-go decision on the expensive run, which today is typically a single point estimate.
