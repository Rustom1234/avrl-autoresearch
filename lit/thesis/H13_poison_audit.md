# H13: Budgeted Defensive Auditing of Training Data for Poisoning and Backdoors

## 1. Hypothesis and SOFisher mapping

Poisoned or backdoor-triggering documents in a pretraining corpus are rare, expensive to confirm (gradient-based influence on a trigger behaviour, or retraining-based detection), and cluster by source, near-duplicate template, or trigger phrase, so a learned policy that spends the expensive test where cheap signals and earlier hits point should find poison clusters in far fewer tests than uniform or static-ranked auditing. This mirrors SOFisher (Ng et al., Nature Communications, May 2026, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-73404-6): imaging budget becomes audit budget, field-of-view choice becomes corpus-region choice, spatial autocorrelation of rare targets becomes source/duplication/template clustering of poison, and the costly fluorescence measurement becomes the costly influence or retraining-based test.

## 2. Prior art

1. Carlini et al., "Poisoning Web-Scale Training Datasets is Practical," IEEE S&P 2024, arXiv:2302.10149 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10149). $60 sufficed to poison roughly 0.01% of LAION-400M or COYO-700M via split-view and frontrunning attacks.
2. Souly, Rando, Chapman, Davies et al. (Anthropic, UK AI Security Institute, Alan Turing Institute), "Poisoning Attacks on LLMs Require a Near-Constant Number of Poison Samples," 2025, arXiv:2510.07192 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07192). 250 poisoned documents reliably backdoored models from 600M to 13B parameters trained on 6B-260B tokens, independent of dataset size.
3. Zhang et al., "Persistent Pre-Training Poisoning of LLMs," 2024, arXiv:2410.13722 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13722). Poisoning just 0.1% of pretraining data made 3 of 4 attack objectives persist after fine-tuning; belief manipulation reached 99.9% attack success.
4. Hubinger et al. (Anthropic), "Sleeper Agents: Training Deceptive LLMs that Persist Through Safety Training," 2024, arXiv:2401.05566 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05566). Backdoor behaviour survived SFT, RL fine-tuning, and adversarial training, most persistently in the largest models tested.
5. Tran, Li, Madry, "Spectral Signatures in Backdoor Attacks," NeurIPS 2018 (https://papers.neurips.cc/paper/8024-spectral-signatures-in-backdoor-attacks.pdf). Removing examples flagged by the top singular-value correlation cut backdoored misclassification to within 1% of a clean-trained baseline.
6. Chen et al., "Detecting Backdoor Attacks on Deep Neural Networks by Activation Clustering," 2018, arXiv:1811.03728 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.03728). k=2 clustering of last-layer activations separates poisoned from clean examples without needing a trusted clean reference set.
7. Xu et al., "Detecting AI Trojans Using Meta Neural Analysis (MNTD)," 2019, arXiv:1910.03137 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.03137). Retraining-based shadow-model classifier achieves 97% detection AUC and generalizes to attack types unseen during shadow training; this is the retraining-based expensive test in the H13 framing.
8. "Delta-Influence: Unlearning Poisons via Influence Functions," 2024, arXiv:2411.13731 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13731). Traces poisoned training data back from a single poisoned test example by detecting an "influence collapse" signal after data transformation.
9. "Detecting Instruction Fine-tuning Attacks on Language Models with Influence Function," 2025, arXiv:2504.09026 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09026). Removing the top 1% of examples flagged by influence-under-semantic-transformation restored near-clean model performance.
10. Gallagher, Esfandiari, MacPhee, Warren, "Provably effective detection of effective data poisoning attacks," 2025, arXiv:2501.11795 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11795). Proves a polynomial-time statistical test (Conformal Separability Test) that detects poisoning with probability better than random guessing whenever the poisoning is strong enough to be effective, an information-theoretic strength-detectability tradeoff.
11. Lee et al., "Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better," 2021, arXiv:2107.06499 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.06499). MinHash-based near-duplicate clustering removes large fractions of a web corpus (UNVERIFIED exact percentage for the specific dataset variant cited across sources; commonly reported in the 20-60% range depending on corpus) and is the standard cheap duplication feature used as an auditing signal.
12. Chen et al. (PoisonBench), "PoisonBench: Assessing Large Language Model Vulnerability to Data Poisoning," 2024, arXiv:2410.08811 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08811). Standardized benchmark showing vulnerability scales with model parameters and is insensitive to a wide range of poisoning ratios across 21 models, providing a reusable injected-poison testbed.
13. "DQS: A Low-Budget Query Strategy for Enhancing Unsupervised Data-driven Anomaly Detection Approaches," 2025, arXiv:2509.05663 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05663). A dissimilarity-based query strategy for spending a small oracle-label budget outperforms baseline query strategies specifically in the small-budget regime, the general budgeted-auditing analog of H13 outside the LLM setting.
14. "Large Language Model Sourcing: A Survey," 2025, arXiv:2510.10161 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10161). Surveys posterior data-attribution methods (influence functions, memorization) versus prior provenance methods (fingerprinting); notes gradient-based attribution has been scaled to retrieve influential examples for an 8B-parameter model from a 160B-token pretraining corpus, giving a cost reference for the expensive test at scale.

## 3. What is genuinely open as of 2026

No paper found couples a sequential, learning decision policy to the poison-detection expensive test. Existing defenses are static single-pass filters (spectral signatures, activation clustering), fixed-budget benchmarks (PoisonBench), one-shot statistical tests (Gallagher et al.), or influence-function passes run globally rather than adaptively targeted (Delta-Influence, arXiv:2504.09026). The near-constant-poison-count result (arXiv:2510.07192) establishes that as few as 250 documents can backdoor any size model, which means the ratio of expensive-test budget to corpus size that a defender can afford keeps shrinking as corpora grow, exactly the regime where a sequential policy should beat uniform sampling; but no cited 2025-2026 work measures detections-per-expensive-test as a function of an adaptive versus static or uniform test allocation on injected LLM-pretraining poison. UNVERIFIED: whether poison clustering in real pretraining corpora (by source domain, near-duplicate template, or trigger phrase) is strong enough at realistic 250-document scale, spread across a multi-trillion-token corpus, for a policy to exploit; SOFisher's spatial autocorrelation is a property of tissue biology, and the analogous claim for poison injection patterns is plausible from the split-view/frontrunning attack descriptions in Carlini et al. (arXiv:2302.10149) but not directly measured anywhere found. A year is needed to (a) build or source labeled poison-injected corpora at multiple scales, (b) establish that cheap features (dedup, source, anomaly score) correlate with poison location enough to be useful priors without already solving detection, and (c) show the sequential policy's gain holds under adversaries who deliberately de-cluster or camouflage injections once they know auditing is sequential.

## 4. Three-condition check

1. Targets are rare: confirmed. 250 documents suffice regardless of corpus size (arXiv:2510.07192); 0.1% or less also suffices for several attack objectives (arXiv:2410.13722); Carlini et al. poisoned roughly 0.01% of two web-scale datasets for $60 (arXiv:2302.10149).
2. Targets are structurally clustered: partially confirmed. Split-view and frontrunning poisoning inject through specific purchased domains or specific scheduled edits, a source-level and template-level cluster by construction (arXiv:2302.10149); standard corpus pipelines already build near-duplicate clusters via MinHash that would contain templated poison (arXiv:2107.06499). Direct measurement of poison-cluster strength at attack scale is UNVERIFIED.
3. Expensive test costs far more than cheap features, and cheap features alone cannot rank targets: confirmed for the "cheap features are insufficient" half. 2026 commentary explicitly notes that belief-manipulation and context-extraction poisoning is written in fluent English and evades common filter artifacts (source: scalable poison detection search summary, corroborating arXiv:2510.07192's framing); the expensive tests, shadow-model retraining (arXiv:1910.03137, 97% AUC) or gradient-based attribution at an 8B-model/160B-token scale (arXiv:2510.10161), are costly by construction relative to a duplication or anomaly score lookup.

## 5. Year plan

Phase 1 (weeks 1-6, 4 A100s): inject a known poison set (reuse PoisonBench-style triggers, arXiv:2410.08811, and the belief-manipulation objective from arXiv:2410.13722) into a 125M-parameter pretraining run on a corpus with built-in near-duplicate and source metadata; budget the expensive test as either a fixed-size influence-function pass (adapt arXiv:2504.09026's method) or a small retrained shadow classifier (adapt arXiv:1910.03137). Metric: poisoned documents found per expensive test executed (detections per test, at fixed total test budget), compared against uniform random test allocation and a static rank-once-by-cheap-features baseline. Strongest existing baseline: static ranking by duplication cluster size and anomaly score with no sequential update, run once, since no adaptive sequential baseline exists in the literature surveyed.
Phase 2 (months 2-6): scale corpus and poison count toward the 250-document, multi-billion-token regime of arXiv:2510.07192; test policy robustness when poison is deliberately spread across many unrelated sources to break the clustering assumption.
Phase 3 (months 6-12): transfer to a real or semi-real web-scale corpus slice with provenance metadata (building on arXiv:2510.10161's sourcing survey and arXiv:2506.01732's Common Corpus provenance fields), and red-team the policy against an adversary aware that auditing is sequential.

## 6. Kill criteria

Kill if, in Phase 1, the static cheap-feature ranking baseline matches the sequential policy's detections-per-test within noise, meaning cheap features already rank targets well enough that sequential updating adds nothing (would falsify condition 3). Kill if poison clustering measured directly in Phase 1 data is too weak for the policy to beat uniform allocation by a meaningful margin, meaning condition 2 does not hold at realistic scale. Kill if an adversary in Phase 2 can decluster injections at negligible extra cost to itself, erasing the exploitable structure the policy depends on.

## 7. Impact on a lab's stack

If it works, this becomes a pre-training data-hygiene pass that sits before or alongside existing dedup and quality filtering, letting the lab run its most expensive poison test (influence attribution or shadow retraining) on a small, prioritized fraction of a corpus and still catch clustered poison campaigns like the ones described in Carlini et al. and the near-constant-poison result, protecting the lab's own models without per-document cost that scales with corpus size.
