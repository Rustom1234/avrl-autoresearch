# H4: Active Human-Preference Collection for Reward Models

## 1. Hypothesis and SOFisher Mapping

Human preference labels are the most expensive measurement in the post-training stack, and cheap proxy signals (model log-probs, response length, lexical features, prompt category) cannot by themselves rank which comparisons a reward model most needs. A policy that chooses which prompt-response pairs to send to annotators next, exploiting the fact that reward-model uncertainty and annotator disagreement cluster in prompt space and by response type, and adapting selection as earlier labels arrive, will produce a better reward model and better downstream policy per label than random or uncertainty-only selection.

SOFisher mapping: field of view -> prompt-response pair (or prompt-completion set); rare target -> a comparison that shifts the reward model's decision boundary or resolves high annotator disagreement; expensive measurement -> human preference label; cheap state features -> reward-model logits, ensemble variance, embedding-space density, DPO implicit margins; spatial autocorrelation of targets -> semantic/topical clustering of hard or disagreement-prone prompts in embedding space, mirrored across iterations as the policy improves.

## 2. Prior Art

- Das, Chakraborty, Pacchiano, Chowdhury, "Active Preference Optimization for Sample Efficient RLHF," ICML 2024 workshop / arXiv:2402.10500. Contextual preference bandit formulation with linear Bradley-Terry reward; proves uniform sampling suffers a constant suboptimality gap under a fixed label budget, while APO's gap matches an Omega(d/sqrt(T)) lower bound up to log factors. Code: https://github.com/nirjhar-das/active-preference-optimization
- Muldrew, Hayes, Zhang, Barber, "Active Preference Learning for Large Language Models," ICML 2024, PMLR 235:36577-36590. arXiv:2402.08114. Acquisition function combining predictive entropy of the LM with DPO's implicit preference-model certainty to pick which prompt/completion pairs to label next.
- Mehta, Das, Neopane, et al., "Sample Efficient Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback via Active Exploration," arXiv:2312.00267 (2023). Formalizes preference collection as a dueling contextual bandit with polynomial worst-case sample-complexity guarantees; UNVERIFIED exact empirical win-rate numbers (abstract-level access only).
- "Neural Dueling Bandits" (Verma et al. lineage), ICLR 2025, https://www.mit.edu/~jaillet/general/iclr25.pdf. UCB/Thompson-sampling style algorithms for nonlinear (neural) reward functions under dueling/preference feedback with sub-linear regret guarantees; extends linear dueling-bandit theory to the neural-reward-model setting relevant to LLM RMs.
- "Beyond Numeric Rewards: In-Context Dueling Bandits with LLM Agents," ACL 2025 Findings, https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.519/. Evaluates LLMs as in-context dueling-bandit decision makers; reports an optimality gap versus classic dueling-bandit algorithms (LLMs struggle to converge/exploit) — relevant as a negative result for naive LLM-as-selector baselines.
- Guo et al. lineage, "Improving Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback with Efficient Reward Model Ensemble," arXiv:2401.16635. Uses an ensemble of reward models for uncertainty estimation to improve RLHF training efficiency and robustness; disagreement (ensemble std-dev) used as an OOD/uncertainty signal.
- "Reward Model Overoptimisation in Iterated RLHF," arXiv:2505.18126 (2025). Studies how repeated reward-model retraining rounds in iterated RLHF accumulate overoptimization; directly relevant to whether an active selection policy that shifts label distribution over rounds worsens or mitigates overoptimization.
- Yang, Cui, Cai, Huang, Shi, Lam, "Not All Preference Pairs Are Created Equal: A Recipe for Annotation-Efficient Iterative Preference Learning," EMNLP 2024 Findings, arXiv:2406.17312. Finds that annotating pairs with small DPO-implicit reward margins beats large-margin or random selection, and that front-loading annotation budget in earlier iterations beats spreading it evenly — a concrete, load-bearing prior result for "adapting to what earlier labels revealed."
- "ActiveUltraFeedback: Efficient Preference Data Generation using Active Learning," arXiv:2603.09692 (2026), lasgroup/ETH Zurich, code: https://github.com/lasgroup/ActiveUltraFeedback. Proposes Double Reverse Thompson Sampling (DRTS) and DeltaUCB acquisition functions; matches or beats full-data UltraFeedback baselines with roughly one-sixth of the labeled data; reports 76.70% win rate (Qwen3-235B judge, probabilistic scoring) on downstream DPO/IPO/SimPO-trained models versus random and full UltraFeedback baselines, evaluated on GSM8K, IFEval, TruthfulQA, AlpacaEval 2, RewardBench 2.
- Li, Liu, Zhou, Gui, Weng, Yuan, Wei, Li, "Uncertainty-Aware Iterative Preference Optimization for Enhanced LLM Reasoning" (IUPO), ACL 2025, https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1169/. Confidence-gated iterative preference optimization; reports a 3.6% improvement over standard DPO after three iterations across five reasoning benchmarks plus one self-curated set.
- "Uncertainty-Penalized Direct Preference Optimization," arXiv:2410.20187 (2024). Penalizes DPO updates by estimated preference-model uncertainty rather than selecting labels, i.e., uncertainty used inside the loss rather than for acquisition — a useful contrast baseline for "uncertainty-only" selection framed in the hypothesis.
- "Less is More: Improving LLM Alignment via Preference Data Selection," NeurIPS 2025, arXiv:2502.14560, code: https://github.com/xiangtanshi/DPO-Data-Selection. Iterative DPO with dynamic preference-data selection; UNVERIFIED specific win-rate numbers (repository README did not surface them; full paper not fetched under time budget).
- "From Selection to Generation: A Survey of LLM-based Active Learning," arXiv:2502.11767 (2025). Survey covering the shift from classic pool-based active learning to LLM-in-the-loop selection and generation of training examples, useful for situating preference-specific acquisition functions within the broader active-learning-for-LLMs literature.
- "Adaptive Preference Optimization with Uncertainty-aware Utility Anchor," EMNLP 2025 Findings, arXiv:2509.10515. Anchors preference optimization using an uncertainty-aware utility estimate rather than a fixed margin; another 2025 example of uncertainty entering the objective, distinct from acquisition-time selection.

## 3. What Is Genuinely Open as of 2026, and Why a Year Is Needed

The prior art establishes three separate pieces but not their combination at production scale: (a) theoretical dueling-bandit and contextual-preference-bandit guarantees (Das, Mehta, Neural Dueling Bandits) that assume linear or small neural reward models, far smaller than production RMs trained on 7B-70B backbones; (b) practical acquisition heuristics (Muldrew's entropy+certainty, ActiveUltraFeedback's DRTS/DeltaUCB, margin-based selection in "Not All Preference Pairs") validated on single-round or few-round offline pools, not on a genuinely online, multi-round pipeline where the reward model, the policy, and the prompt distribution all shift together; and (c) evidence that naive LLM-as-bandit-agent selection underperforms classical algorithms (ACL 2025 dueling-bandits paper), meaning an LLM cannot simply be asked to pick informative pairs — a purpose-built policy is required. No published work combines spatial-style clustering of disagreement in embedding space (the direct SOFisher analog of tissue autocorrelation) with a learned, budget-aware next-pair selector that adapts across RLHF/DPO training rounds and is evaluated against both random and uncertainty-only baselines on the same iterative pipeline. Closing this gap needs a year because it requires: building/simulating an annotator oracle at scale, running multiple full RLHF or iterative-DPO training loops (each loop is a multi-GPU-day cost) to get round-over-round data rather than one-shot pool results, and testing whether gains that show up in small-model dueling-bandit theory and single-round LLM demos survive compounding reward-model drift over many rounds — exactly the overoptimization risk flagged in the Iterated RLHF paper above.

## 4. Three-Condition Check

1. Spatial (embedding-space) autocorrelation of the expensive signal: "Not All Preference Pairs Are Created Equal" shows DPO implicit-margin structure clusters informative pairs together and that front-loading budget in early iterations wins, implying informativeness is not i.i.d. across the prompt/response space (arXiv:2406.17312).
2. Expensive measurement dominates cost relative to cheap features: human annotation cost per comparison vastly exceeds the cost of computing reward-model logits, ensemble variance, or DPO margins, which is the explicit motivation in Das et al. (constant suboptimality gap under budget constraint) and Mehta et al.'s dueling-bandit framing (arXiv:2402.10500, arXiv:2312.00267).
3. Cheap features alone cannot rank targets: the ACL 2025 dueling-bandits paper shows LLM agents (a cheap, feature-only proxy) fail to match classical bandit algorithms at exploiting preference structure, and Uncertainty-Penalized DPO shows uncertainty folded into the loss (not used for selection) is a different, weaker lever than acquisition-time selection — together indicating a learned selection policy is needed, not a fixed heuristic on cheap signals alone.

## 5. Year Plan

Phase 1 (weeks 1-6, first experiment on 4 A100s in under a week): Use an existing public preference dataset (UltraFeedback or Anthropic HH-RLHF) with a simulated Bradley-Terry annotator (oracle scores from a larger frozen judge model, e.g., a held-out reward model, standing in for a human labeler) so no real annotation is needed. Train a small reward model (Llama-3-8B or similar) under three acquisition policies with a fixed label budget per round: random, uncertainty-only (ensemble disagreement, per ActiveUltraFeedback/reward-ensemble prior art), and a learned selector (contextual bandit or lightweight DQN over prompt-embedding-space clusters plus RM disagreement features). Metric: reward-model accuracy on a held-out oracle-labeled test set, and downstream policy win-rate after one DPO round, both plotted as a function of label budget (labels-to-accuracy curve). Strongest existing baseline to beat: ActiveUltraFeedback's DRTS/DeltaUCB (reports parity at ~1/6 of full-data labels) — the learned selector must beat DRTS/DeltaUCB at matched budget, not just beat random.
Phase 2 (months 2-6): Extend to multi-round iterative DPO/RLHF (3-5 rounds), where the selector adapts as the reward model and policy drift, and measure whether gains compound or decay (testing against the overoptimization risk in arXiv:2505.18126). Add real low-budget human annotation (crowd or in-house) for one full run to validate the simulated-annotator results transfer.
Phase 3 (months 6-12): Scale to a production-sized reward model and backbone, integrate with an existing RLHF/DPO pipeline, and run a held-out A/B against the lab's current random/heuristic sampling on a live annotation queue, reporting label-efficiency and downstream policy quality gains.

## 6. Kill Criteria

- If, at matched label budget, the learned selector does not beat DRTS/DeltaUCB (ActiveUltraFeedback) and margin-based selection ("Not All Preference Pairs") by a margin larger than run-to-run variance across 3+ seeds, the added policy complexity is not justified.
- If gains present in round 1 vanish or reverse by round 3-5 of iterative DPO (i.e., active selection accelerates reward-model overoptimization rather than mitigating it), the method fails the compounding-drift test central to the SOFisher analogy.
- If the simulated-annotator results do not transfer when validated against a small real-human-annotation run (effect size drops by more than half), the oracle assumption is invalid and the approach needs redesign before scaling.

## 7. Impact: What a Lab Would Change

A lab currently sending prompt-response pairs to annotators via random sampling or a static uncertainty filter would instead route pairs through a learned, continuously retrained selection policy sitting between generation and the annotation queue: it would re-rank the candidate pool after every batch of returned labels (not just once per round), prioritize pairs from embedding-space clusters currently showing high reward-model disagreement, and reallocate a larger share of the fixed weekly labeling budget to early training iterations rather than spreading it evenly. Concretely, this changes annotation-pipeline software (an online scoring/ranking service in front of the labeling queue) and changes reward-model retraining cadence (more frequent, budget-aware retraining tied to selector updates rather than fixed-size batch collection).
