# K1: Adversarial kill attempt on H1 (Active Evaluation During Training)

## Three closest works

1. **ATLAS**, arXiv 2511.04689, https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04689. Fisher-information-guided IRT item selection reduces required items by up to 90%; matches whole-item-bank ability estimate on HellaSwag using 41 of 5,600 items (MAE 0.157). Confirmed by direct abstract fetch. Operates per checkpoint independently; the abstract does not describe or claim cross-checkpoint reuse (UNVERIFIED whether the full paper tests it, abstract silent).

2. **Active Evaluation Acquisition for Efficient LLM Benchmarking**, arXiv 2410.05952 (ICML 2025), https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05952. RL-based policy picks the next test item by modeling dependencies "across test examples," i.e. at a single evaluation snapshot. Confirmed by direct abstract fetch: dependencies are across items, not across time/checkpoints. Quantitative efficiency number not stated in the abstract itself (UNVERIFIED exact %).

3. **Leveraging computerized adaptive testing for cost-effective evaluation of LLMs in medical benchmarking**, npj Digital Medicine 2026, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-026-02671-w. Near-perfect correlation with full item bank using 1.3% of items; eval time 6.85 hours to 8.4 minutes; cost ~$1,475 to under $5. One-shot CAT at a single time point, not a scheduler across training checkpoints.

A targeted arXiv abstract search for `"checkpoint" AND "regression detection" AND "language model"` returned zero results (query run 2026-09-22 via export.arxiv.org API), and a search for `"checkpoint" AND "evaluation" AND "scheduler" AND "language model"` surfaced no paper doing cross-checkpoint adaptive item selection, only adjacent work like HarnessBandit (arXiv, 2026, exact ID UNVERIFIED), an online bandit that schedules RL training harnesses using sliding-window learnability signals, not evaluation items. This is suggestive, not proof of absence.

## Strongest reason a committee kills this

Not "already published" (the literal cross-checkpoint combination did not turn up). The stronger attack: **the stated Impact-section goal is already achievable today by trivially looping ATLAS or the medical-CAT method at every checkpoint**, no new research needed. ATLAS alone gets 90% item reduction and the medical-CAT paper gets 1.3%-of-items near-perfect correlation, both far cheaper than the "hundreds to thousands of dollars per sweep" baseline the thesis cites. A committee will ask why a naive "run ATLAS every N steps" baseline isn't sufficient, and the thesis's own kill criterion (a) concedes this exact risk: if a static per-checkpoint IRT baseline matches the learned policy within noise, the thesis dies in week 6.

## Verdict

A year-long thesis has a defensible gap, but it is narrower than section 3 claims. The genuinely unpublished piece is not "adaptive item selection during training" (solved, per above) but specifically: does modeling checkpoint-to-checkpoint score autocorrelation beat a strong independent-per-checkpoint ATLAS baseline by a non-trivial margin, under a winner's-curse-calibrated stopping rule, on judge-based (not just exact-match) benchmarks. This is falsifiable cheaply, which is a strength, but also means the thesis could legitimately die at week 6 rather than month 12, which the committee will flag as a thin margin for a "year-long" commitment.
