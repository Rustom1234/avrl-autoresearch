# H12: Budgeted Benchmark-Contamination Search in Pretraining Corpora

## 1. Hypothesis and SOFisher mapping

A learned policy that decides which corpus regions (documents, host/domain clusters) to send next to an expensive semantic or LLM-based contamination matcher, exploiting host-level clustering of contamination and the hit pattern observed so far, will surface more contaminated documents per expensive call than static ranking (frequency prior, embedding-similarity top-K, or exhaustive) and can run continuously as a pre-training-run audit. This maps to SOFisher's field-of-view selection: benchmark test items are the "rare targets," corpus documents (or host clusters) are "fields of view," cheap n-gram/embedding features are the cheap state, and the LLM/semantic matcher is the expensive per-shot measurement whose budget is limited.

## 2. Prior art

- OpenAI GPT-4 Technical Report, 2023. https://cdn.openai.com/papers/gpt-4.pdf — reports per-benchmark n-gram (up to 40-gram) contamination checks; e.g. AP US History MCQ shows 73% contamination overlap by their count, with negligible score delta on the clean subset.
- Touvron et al., Llama 2, 2023 (as summarized in secondary sources; primary text not directly fetched this pass). https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288 — reported roughly 12-18% n-gram overlap on MMLU depending on model size (7B vs 70B). UNVERIFIED exact split, sourced from a secondary blog synthesis, not confirmed against the primary PDF in this pass.
- Sainz et al., "NLP Evaluation in Trouble: On the Need to Measure LLM Data Contamination for Each Benchmark," EMNLP Findings 2023 (blog: "Did ChatGPT cheat on your test?"). https://github.com/hitz-zentroa/lm-contamination — GPT-4 exact-matched the withheld answer option on MMLU cloze reconstruction 57% of the time (ChatGPT 52%), evidence of memorization beyond chance.
- Yang, Chiang, Zheng, Gonzalez, Stoica, "Rethinking Benchmark and Contamination for Language Models with Rephrased Samples," 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04850 — shows a 13B model trained on GPT-4-rephrased/translated test items reaches accuracy "on par with GPT-4" on MMLU/GSM8K/HumanEval while passing standard n-gram decontamination; their LLM Decontaminator then found previously unknown overlap in RedPajama-Data-1T and CodeAlpaca that n-gram filters missed. Exact overlap percentage not confirmed in this pass (UNVERIFIED).
- Deng, Zhao, Tang, Gerstein, Cohan, "Investigating Data Contamination in Modern Benchmarks for Large Language Models," NAACL 2024 (arXiv Nov 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09783 — introduces retrieval-based overlap search plus the TS-Guessing slot-filling protocol for black-box proprietary models; specific contamination rate per benchmark not confirmed in this pass (UNVERIFIED).
- Dekoninck et al., "ConStat: Performance-Based Contamination Detection in Large Language Models," NeurIPS 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16281 — redefines contamination as inflated non-generalizing performance and reports "high levels of contamination" in Mistral, Llama, Yi, and the top-3 Open LLM Leaderboard models at the time; exact quantified rate not confirmed in this pass (UNVERIFIED).
- Shi et al., "Detecting Pretraining Data from Large Language Models" (Min-K% Prob), ICLR 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16789 — introduces the WikiMIA benchmark and the Min-K% Prob likelihood-based detector, reported as the strongest baseline on WikiMIA at publication; exact AUC not confirmed in this pass (UNVERIFIED).
- Elazar et al., "What's In My Big Data?" (WIMBD), ICLR 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20707 — a count-and-search platform over 35+ TB of corpora; documents contamination of the Winograd Schema Challenge and parts of GLUE/SuperGLUE inside RedPajama and C4-family corpora, and separately finds roughly 50% duplicate documents in RedPajama and LAION-2B-en (a related but distinct number, cited for corpus-scale context).
- Zarzecki, Dubiński, Cygert, "The Reliability Gap in Benchmark Auditing: Distribution Shift and Scale as Failure Modes of Contamination Detection," 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03305 — across 335 evaluations spanning 25 models (up to 27B) and three detection paradigms (LLM Dataset Inference, Post-Hoc Dataset Inference, CoDeC), only 201 (60%) yielded correct outcomes; LLM DI gives false positives under distribution shift, Post-Hoc DI is underpowered at benchmark scale, CoDeC gives only coarse provenance.
- "Evading Data Contamination Detection for Language Models is (too) Easy," 2024. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.02823 — shows simple rephrasing/obfuscation defeats standard n-gram and embedding-based decontamination filters; specific evasion success rate not confirmed in this pass (UNVERIFIED).
- "A Survey on Data Contamination for Large Language Models," 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14425 — catalogs corpus sources (CommonCrawl, GitHub, dataset mirrors) as contamination vectors; no single contamination rate, cited for taxonomy.
- "Does Data Contamination Detection Work (Well) for LLMs? A Survey and Evaluation on Detection Assumptions," 2024/2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18966 — stress-tests the assumptions (IID access to clean reference data, sufficient scale) underlying existing detectors, a direct precursor to the 2026 Reliability Gap paper's findings.
- "TRACER: A Semantic-Aware Framework for Fine-Grained Contamination Detection in Code LLMs," 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24079 — 2026 work applying semantic (not string) matching to code-benchmark contamination, one number: framed as fine-grained (function/snippet-level) rather than document-level detection, exact metric not confirmed in this pass.
- "LLM Benchmark Datasets Should Be Contamination-Resistant," 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19999 — argues for contamination-resistant benchmark design as a complement to post-hoc detection; relevant to why detection-side work (this hypothesis) remains necessary even as benchmark design improves.
- "Beyond Surface-Level Similarity: Hierarchical Contamination Detection for Synthetic Training Data in Foundation Models," 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.17602 — proposes a tiered (cheap-then-expensive) detection cascade for synthetic training data, structurally the closest prior work to the budgeted-policy idea in H12, but static/tiered rather than adaptively learned.

## 3. What is genuinely open as of 2026

No paper found in this pass casts contamination search as a sequential decision / bandit problem that adaptively allocates a fixed expensive-matcher budget across corpus regions using host-level or prior-hit signal; the closest is the static hierarchical cascade (Beyond Surface-Level Similarity, 2025). The single load-bearing empirical claim in H12, that contamination clusters detectably by source host or domain (dataset mirrors, tutorial/Q&A sites) in a way a policy can exploit, is plausible from general knowledge of how benchmarks leak (GitHub repos, HuggingFace dataset cards, Quizlet-style study sites) but is UNVERIFIED against any paper directly measuring host-level autocorrelation of contamination rate. Separately, the 2026 Reliability Gap paper shows the underlying statistical detectors that would serve as the "expensive oracle" are themselves unreliable under distribution shift and scale, meaning a policy built on top of an unreliable oracle risks learning to game oracle noise rather than real contamination. A year is needed to (a) first measure whether host-level autocorrelation is real and strong enough to exploit at all, (b) build and validate the adaptive policy against static baselines at meaningful corpus scale, and (c) characterize how oracle unreliability propagates into policy behavior, none of which exists yet.

## 4. Three-condition check

- Spatial/structural autocorrelation of targets: WIMBD documents that contamination is not uniform but concentrated in specific corpora/sources (Winograd/GLUE/SuperGLUE inside RedPajama, C4). This supports clustering by corpus/source but not yet host-level clustering within a single large corpus; treat as partially supported, largely UNVERIFIED at the host granularity H12 needs.
- Expensive measurement costs far more than cheap features: Yang et al. 2023's LLM Decontaminator requires GPT-4 calls per candidate pair after a cheap embedding pre-filter, and the Reliability Gap paper's LLM Dataset Inference / Post-Hoc DI / CoDeC paradigms require reference-model access or held-out statistical fitting, both far costlier than n-gram matching. Supported.
- Cheap features alone cannot rank targets well: Yang et al. 2023's core finding, that n-gram/string matching misses paraphrase and translation entirely, and the "Evading Data Contamination Detection... is (too) Easy" paper both directly support this. Supported.

## 5. Year plan

- Phase 1 (months 1-4): Feasibility and the core measurement. On 4 A100s within the first week, subsample roughly 1-5M documents from a public FineWeb or Dolma snapshot, compute cheap features (10-13 gram hashes plus a small embedding model, e.g. an E5-class encoder, both fit within 4x A100 memory/throughput for a sample this size) against public benchmark items (MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval). Build a host/domain graph from URL metadata. Use a contextual-bandit or Thompson-sampling policy over (host cluster, benchmark) arms, reward = contaminated documents confirmed by an LLM-judge verifier call, under a fixed budget of expensive calls (e.g. 5,000). Metric: contaminated documents found per expensive call, compared at matched budget. Strongest existing baseline: LLM Decontaminator (Yang et al. 2023) applied statically, i.e. rank all cheap-filtered candidates by embedding similarity and spend the same expensive-call budget top-down, with no adaptive host exploitation.
- Phase 2 (months 5-8): Scale to the full public corpus and a broader benchmark set (add BIG-bench subsets, GPQA), test whether host autocorrelation transfers across corpora (Dolma to FineWeb), and compare against the Reliability-Gap-validated detector paradigms (LLM DI, Post-Hoc DI, CoDeC) as alternative expensive oracles, characterizing how their known failure modes affect policy reward signal.
- Phase 3 (months 9-12): Package as a continuous pre-training-run audit tool; run against a real (permission-cleared) large corpus if the lab has one, produce a suspect-host dashboard, and write up the host-autocorrelation measurement as a standalone contribution regardless of policy outcome.

## 6. Kill criteria

- If Phase 1's measurement shows host-level contamination autocorrelation is weak (policy cannot beat static top-K ranking by a meaningful margin, e.g. under 20% more contaminated docs per call), kill the adaptive-policy framing; the host-clustering premise, the one truly novel and unverified piece, has failed.
- If the LLM-judge or statistical oracle's noise (per the Reliability Gap paper's distribution-shift failure mode) dominates the reward signal such that the policy cannot distinguish real hits from oracle false positives, kill or pivot to fixing oracle reliability first, since a policy on top of a broken oracle is not a useful contribution.
- If a static hierarchical cascade (per Beyond Surface-Level Similarity, 2025) matches the adaptive policy's hits-per-call at the same budget, kill the "adaptive" claim and fold results into a simpler cascade recommendation.

## 7. Impact on a lab's stack

If it works, this becomes a pre-training-run gate: a continuously updated, budget-capped contamination audit that a lab runs before reporting benchmark numbers, producing a ranked list of suspect hosts/documents and a confidence estimate per benchmark, cheaper per contaminated-document-found than exhaustive LLM-based matching and more thorough than n-gram-only checks like those in the GPT-4 and Llama reports. It would also produce, as a byproduct, the host-autocorrelation dataset itself, useful independent of the policy for future decontamination filtering.
