# S01: Cost structure of RL post-training (GRPO / RLVR / RLAIF)

## What this angle contributes to the thesis

This angle establishes that rollout generation, reward/verification, and the policy update are three distinct, separately engineered cost centers in modern RL post-training pipelines, which is the factual premise AVRL depends on. Systems papers (OpenRLHF, HybridFlow/verl, ROSE) confirm engineers already treat generation and verification as separable bottlenecks worth independent optimization, and algorithm papers (GRPO, DAPO, TTRL) show that not all rollouts or rollout groups deserve equal compute, which is the prompt-space heterogeneity AVRL wants to exploit. The evidence gathered here is largely indirect: no frontier lab report retrieved in this pass discloses a dollar or GPU-hour breakdown of rollout cost versus reward/judge cost, so the "verifier is the dominant cost" premise is argued by inference from systems engineering choices, not confirmed by a direct accounting.

## What supports the thesis, what threatens it

Supports: sources 5 and 4 show generation and policy update are already engineered as distinct, separately optimized cost centers, consistent with treating verification as a third, schedulable cost center. Sources 9 and 11 show fixed thresholds and cascades already recover most of the benefit of tiered verification, so AVRL's claim to beat a fixed threshold has a real, nontrivial baseline to clear rather than a strawman. Source 10 (VIP) shows a learned, per-prompt budget allocator already beats uniform or heuristic allocation in a closely analogous problem, rollout budget rather than verifier-tier budget, supporting the viability of AVRL's mechanism class. Source 7 (TTRL) shows the cheap-proxy end of the spectrum, majority vote with zero verification cost, can produce very large gains alone, so the proxy AVRL might substitute for an expensive verifier has real standalone value. Source 2's (DAPO) dynamic sampling, which filters zero-gradient prompt groups before spending further compute, is itself evidence that prompt-space heterogeneity is a known, exploitable signal in current practice.

Threatens: source 11's core finding, that a static cost-stratified threshold captures most of the evaluation-time optimization gains, is a direct empirical threat: if a cheap static rule closes most of the gap, the marginal value of a learned, in-training allocator may be small, and AVRL needs a mechanism-specific argument for why training-time adaptation adds more than a static rule can. Source 14's (ROSE) finding that rollout generation itself, not verification, is often the dominant and highly variable cost in agentic, multi-turn RL narrows the scope where AVRL's core premise cleanly holds; it may apply most strongly to RLAIF or code-sandbox-heavy pipelines where the verifier call is expensive relative to generation, and less to pure RLVR with cheap rule-based verifiers where rollout generation itself is the bottleneck. None of the flagship 2025 to 2026 RL reports retrieved here (sources 1, 3, 2, 8) disclose a rollout-versus-reward-compute cost breakdown in their abstracts (UNVERIFIED beyond abstract-level access), so the foundational claim that "the scorer is the dominant cost" rests on inference from systems papers rather than a confirmed dollar or GPU-hour accounting from any frontier lab. Finally, source 6's (GRPO) own motivation was to cut memory and compute on the policy-update side by removing the critic, a reminder that RL post-training cost already splits into at least three distinct bottlenecks (policy-update memory, rollout generation, reward or verification), and the thesis needs to state precisely which one AVRL targets rather than treating "RL post-training cost" as one undifferentiated quantity.

## Closest prior work from this angle

The closest prior work from this angle is VIP, adaptive rollout allocation (source 10, ICLR 2026, arXiv:2602.01601). It is the only source here that is, like AVRL, a learned, per-prompt-group budget allocator operating during RL training itself, rather than a fixed rule applied at evaluation time (sources 9, 11) or a cost-saving architecture change applied uniformly to every rollout (sources 4, 5, 6). It differs from AVRL in the object being allocated and the quantity protected: VIP decides how many rollouts to sample per prompt to minimize expected policy-gradient variance, treating every sampled rollout's reward as trustworthy once computed, whereas AVRL decides, for an already-generated group of rollouts, whether to pay for an expensive, trustworthy verifier or accept a cheap, exploitable proxy, and explicitly tracks reward corruption and adversarial exploitation of the proxy as the thing being optimized against. VIP's budget currency is rollout count; AVRL's is verifier calls. VIP is the nearest neighbor in mechanism, a learned, budget-capped, training-time allocator over prompt groups, while leaving the verification-cost-and-exploitability axis, AVRL's central claim, unaddressed.
