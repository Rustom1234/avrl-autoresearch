# S06: Theory and Algorithms for Budgeted Measurement (Active Search, Costly Bandits, Experimental Design)

## 1. What this angle contributes to the thesis

Active search, knowledge-gradient policies, and Bayesian experimental design supply the formal language for "is this costly measurement worth its price," which is the exact decision the AVRL allocator makes per rollout group. The active-search literature already demonstrates the thesis's core empirical claim in a cousin domain: nonmyopic, budget-capped policies that exploit correlation structure between candidates (spatial in tissue imaging, prompt-space in AVRL) outperform greedy or uniform sampling at a fixed query count. Bandit theory (best-arm identification, bandits with knapsacks) supplies the regret and sample-complexity machinery needed to state precisely what "beats a fixed threshold" can and cannot mean, and to bound how large the AVRL allocator's edge is allowed to be before the claim becomes implausible.

5. Mes, Powell, Frazier (2011), https://doi.org/10.5555/1953048.2078200: hierarchical KG extends the policy to correlated/aggregated information sources, closer to "clusters" of prompts; 90 citations.
6. Rainforth, Foster, Ivanova, Bickford Smith (2024), Statistical Science, https://doi.org/10.1214/23-sts915: survey unifying Bayesian experimental design as expected-information-gain maximization, including amortized/learned design policies for costly experiments; 99 citations.
7. Badanidiyuru, Kleinberg, Slivkins (2013/2018), FOCS / JACM, https://doi.org/10.1145/3164539: "Bandits with Knapsacks": general framework for bandit learning under a hard budget/resource constraint, O(√T) regret with a matching lower bound; 140 citations (JACM version).
8. Garivier, Kaufmann (2016), COLT, https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.1602.04589: "Track-and-Stop": asymptotically optimal sample complexity for best-arm identification at fixed confidence; 91 citations. Gives the hard floor any budgeted allocator is bounded by.
9. Kossen, Farquhar, Gal, Rainforth (2021), https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2103.05331: "Active Testing": unbiased importance-weighted acquisition cuts the number of labels needed to estimate held-out model performance versus i.i.d. sampling.
10. Tomanek, Hahn (2010), "A Comparison of Models for Cost-Sensitive Active Learning": compares learned cost-prediction models for active learning against simpler heuristics; reports the learned models do not reliably win (25 citations, OpenAlex; no DOI indexed).
11. Persello, Schölkopf (2014), IEEE TGRS, https://doi.org/10.1109/tgrs.2014.2300189: cost-sensitive active learning with lookahead for remote-sensing field surveys; a KG-style lookahead rule under a labeling budget; 46 citations.
12. Badanidiyuru, Kleinberg, Slivkins family extension: Agrawal, Devanur (2014), https://doi.org/10.1145/2600057.2602844: "Bandits with concave rewards and convex knapsacks," generalizes BwK toward multi-resource, nonlinear budget constraints; 124 citations.
13. ## 3. What supports the thesis claim, and what threatens it

Supports: the active-search results (sources 1–3) are the strongest structural analog available: they are literally budget-capped, sequential policies that decide which of many correlated candidates to pay a costly, noisy query for, and the nonmyopic/learned variant beats greedy at equal budget by exploiting exactly the correlation structure the thesis calls "prompt-space clusters." KG theory (4–5, 11) formalizes why a one-step value-of-information rule already strictly dominates naive fixed-frequency sampling in expectation, giving the allocator a principled target to approximate rather than a heuristic. Active Testing (9) is direct precedent for "spend the budget where it changes the estimate most, not where it's cheapest," which is the allocator's job description.

Threatens: best-arm-identification lower bounds (8) mean there is a hard information-theoretic floor on how much any allocator (learned or not) can reduce expensive-call counts for a given confidence target; if the thesis's "beats a fixed threshold" claim is read as unbounded, this angle caps it. More pointed, source 14 (Heteroskedastic Signals) is a live 2026 result arguing the opposite of the thesis's central premise from inside the same problem: it finds a *static*, cost-stratified threshold already captures most of the benefit, and frames the remaining gain (up to 17pp) as bounded and regime-dependent rather than open-ended. Source 10 (Tomanek & Hahn) adds an empirical caution from classical active learning: learned cost/utility models for query selection frequently fail to beat simpler heuristics in practice, which is a direct precedent for "the learned allocator doesn't actually beat the threshold" as a plausible negative result, not just a strawman.

## 4. Closest prior work from this angle, and how it differs

The single closest prior work from this angle is Jiang, Malkomes, Moseley, Garnett's nonmyopic active search line (sources 2–3), which SOFisher itself descends from. It already solves "pick where to spend a scarce, costly, noisy query given correlation structure over candidates, under a hard budget": structurally the same problem AVRL poses for verifier calls. It differs from AVRL in three load-bearing ways. First, active search assumes a fixed pool with a known or separately-learned correlation/generative model (a Gaussian process or similar) over static candidates; AVRL's "candidates" are rollout groups from a policy that is itself changing during training, so the correlation structure the allocator must exploit is nonstationary and partly caused by the very training it is budgeting for. Second, active search's oracle (the assay, the label) is assumed noisy but not adversarial; AVRL's cheap proxy (majority vote, small reward model) can be actively exploited by the co-trained policy, which is a reward-hacking dynamic with no analog in the active-search setting. Third, active search's unit of decision is "which single candidate to query next" from a global budget; AVRL nests the decision inside a per-group, per-training-step allocation embedded in a policy-gradient update, so the allocator's own error propagates into the gradient signal rather than only into an estimate.
