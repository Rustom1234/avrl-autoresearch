# S03: Reward model error structure, calibration, uncertainty, overoptimization (2022-2026)

## 1. What this angle contributes to AVRL

Reward model overoptimization work establishes that proxy reward error is not noise but a structured, predictable function of optimization pressure (KL distance from the reference policy) and reward model capacity, which is the same kind of structure an AVRL allocator would need to learn to decide when a cheap proxy is safe. The ensemble and uncertainty-estimation literature shows that reward error is heteroskedastic across inputs, disagreement or ensemble variance is a usable (if imperfect) signal for when a proxy is exploitable, and static ensembling mitigates but does not eliminate hacking, leaving room for a learned, per-prompt allocator. The calibration and prompt-type literature (length bias, dialect bias, multilingual gaps, RewardBench category breakdowns) directly evidences the "proxy error clusters in prompt space" premise the thesis needs, by showing reward models fail unevenly across identifiable input types rather than uniformly at random.

## 2. Sources

1. Gao, Schulman, Hilton, 2022, arXiv preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760: gold-reward score vs. proxy-reward-model score follows a smooth, predictable functional form in sqrt(KL) from the reference policy for both best-of-n and RL, and larger reward models show less severe overoptimization at matched KL.
2. Coste, Anwar, Kirk, Krueger, 2023, arXiv preprint (ICLR 2024), https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02743: conservative aggregation over a reward model ensemble (e.g., worst-case-over-members) delays the onset of overoptimization versus a single proxy reward model, but does not eliminate it as KL budget grows.
3. Moskovitz, Singh, Strouse, Sandholm, Salakhutdinov, Dragan, McAleer, 2023, arXiv preprint (ICLR 2024), https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04373: correlation between component reward models in an ensemble significantly shifts where overoptimization sets in, motivating constrained RL that caps each reward component instead of trusting an aggregate.
4. Rafailov et al., 2024, arXiv preprint (NeurIPS 2024), https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02900: direct alignment algorithms (e.g., DPO) show their own overoptimization curves in sqrt(KL) distinct from classic RLHF, meaning proxy-error structure is method-dependent, not universal to PPO-style RL.
5. Miao, Zhang, Ding, Bao, Zhang, Tao, 2024, arXiv preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09345: InfoRM uses a variational information bottleneck plus a Cluster Separation Index to detect reward hacking onset directly from the reward model's own representation, tested from 70M to 7B parameter scales.
6. Zhang, Ton, Shen, Wang, Liu, 2024, arXiv preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05171: AdvPO uses lightweight per-sample uncertainty estimation plus distributionally robust optimization against the reward model's confidence region, rather than a fixed ensemble penalty.
7. Leng, Huang, Zhu, Huang, 2024, arXiv preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09724: standard RLHF makes LLMs more overconfident (verbalized and logit confidence rise even as accuracy does not), and two calibration-aware PPO variants (PPO-M, PPO-C) reduce calibration error without extra labeled data.
8. Shen, Chen, Song, Jin, Peng, Mi, Khashabi, Yu, 2023, arXiv preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16155: reward model inconsistency (same content, different scores under superficial perturbation) has a measurable "trickle-down" effect that degrades the downstream RLHF-tuned policy, not just the reward model's own benchmark score.
9. Huang, Qiu, Wang, Ponti, Titov, 2024, arXiv preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.17407: post-hoc calibration that removes length bias yields a 3.11-point average performance gain across 33 reward models on RewardBench, with no additional reward-model training required.
10. Lambert et al., 2024, arXiv preprint (NAACL Findings 2025), https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13787: RewardBench decomposes reward-model accuracy by category (chat, reasoning, safety, adversarial) and shows accuracy varies sharply across these prompt-type buckets for the same model, the clearest direct evidence of prompt-space error clustering.
11. Malik, Pyatkin, Land, Morrison, Smith, Hajishirzi, Lambert, 2025, arXiv preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01937: RewardBench 2, a harder/less-contaminated successor benchmark, finds reward models score roughly 20 points lower on average than on the original RewardBench, meaning today's proxy reward models are less reliable out-of-distribution than benchmark numbers suggest.
12. Gureja, Miranda, Islam, Maheshwary, Sharma, Winata, Lambert, Ruder, Hooker, Fadaee, 2024, arXiv preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15522: M-RewardBench finds a substantial reward-model accuracy gap between English and non-English prompts across 23 languages, tracking translation quality and language-resource availability, another prompt-type axis of proxy failure.
13. Kadavath et al. (Anthropic), 2022, arXiv preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221: LLMs can be trained to self-predict "P(True)" on their own answers with meaningful calibration (AUROC often well above chance) generalizing across task types, the methodological basis much reward-model-calibration work builds on.

## 3. Support vs. threat to the thesis claim

Supports: Gao et al. and the overoptimization-scaling-laws line establish that proxy reward error grows in a structured, non-random way with optimization pressure, so a policy that learns this structure in-training (rather than assuming a fixed threshold) has real signal to exploit, which is the mechanistic justification for "learns during training where the proxy is exploitable." RewardBench, M-RewardBench, and the dialect-bias paper directly support "exploits structure in prompt space," since they show proxy failure is clustered by category, language, and dialect rather than spread uniformly, exactly the clustering an allocator would learn to route around. The ensemble literature (Coste, Moskovitz, AdvPO, the LoRA-ensemble IPM paper) confirms uncertainty/disagreement signals are informative but imperfect, which is consistent with a learned allocator outperforming a static ensemble-variance threshold.

Threatens: nearly all of this literature treats the reward model itself as the object being fixed or penalized at training time, not as a per-rollout binary "pay for verifier vs. accept proxy" decision under an explicit budget, so none of it is a direct existence proof that a learned allocator beats a fixed threshold at equal expensive-call budget; that comparison still has to be made from scratch. The calibration-miscalibration finding (Leng et al.) is a risk to the thesis in a different way: if RLHF training itself pushes reward/confidence signals toward overconfidence as optimization proceeds, an allocator that partly trusts learned uncertainty signals from the reward side could be gamed by the same policy pressure it is trying to detect, i.e., the allocator's own trust signal is a new attack surface. AdvPO and InfoRM show cheap, training-time uncertainty or information-theoretic signals can substitute for expensive ensembles, which weakens the case that expensive-verifier calls are irreplaceable, and slightly undercuts the framing that verification cost is unavoidable rather than engineerable away.

## 4. Closest prior work from this angle

Coste et al. (2023), "Reward Model Ensembles Help Mitigate Overoptimization," https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02743, is the closest work from this angle. It uses multiple reward models and a conservative aggregation rule (not a single proxy) to resist overoptimization, which is structurally adjacent to AVRL's "when is the proxy trustworthy" question. It differs from the thesis on every axis that matters: it aggregates reward signals rather than routing between a cheap proxy and an expensive verifier, it is static (the aggregation rule is fixed, not learned during training as the policy shifts), it has no explicit expensive-call budget constraint, and it evaluates success purely via how far overoptimization is delayed, not via corrupted-reward rate or cost-matched comparison against a threshold baseline.
