# S08: Verifier Engineering and Its Cost

## 1. What this angle contributes to the thesis

This angle establishes the empirical foundation for the AVRL claim that the verifier is both the dominant cost and the dominant attack surface in RLVR. Verifiers range from cheap and structurally cheatable (majority vote, rule-based answer matching) to expensive and still imperfect (generative verifiers, process reward models, code sandboxes, formal proof checkers), and each type has a documented failure mode: reward hacking on spurious signals, false negatives that punish correct answers, and inconsistency under trivial input perturbation. The 2025-2026 literature shows the field already treats verification as a scarce, fallible resource to be allocated (fixed cascades, static cost-stratified thresholds) rather than a free oracle, which is the premise AVRL needs, but nobody has made the allocator itself learn and exploit prompt-space structure during training.

## 3. What supports the thesis claim, and what threatens it

Supports: TinyV and "Where the Verifier Fails" show that even the cheapest deployed verifiers have large, structured error rates (double-digit-percent false negatives, self-consistency swings tied to superficial features like whitespace), exactly the "proxy error clusters in prompt space" the allocator must learn to detect. Spurious Rewards shows the failure is not hypothetical: policies already exploit weak/noisy reward signals in real RLVR runs, grounding the "reward-hacking surface" claim. Large Language Monkeys and the test-time-compute scaling paper both show verification, not generation, becomes the marginal bottleneck as sampling scales, supporting the premise that verifier cost dominates. Trust or Escalate and Heteroskedastic Signals both show a fixed, non-learned allocation rule captures most available gain, which sets the exact baseline AVRL must beat, and both leave a static-threshold ceiling a learned, in-training allocator is positioned to exceed.

Threatens: Heteroskedastic Signals is the sharper threat, its finding is that structural heterogeneity limits how much any optimization, learned or not, can gain beyond a well-chosen static or gated threshold; if that generalizes to in-training RLVR verification, AVRL's edge over a fixed threshold could be small. Trust or Escalate already reaches about 80% coverage at about 80% agreement with a simple, provable, non-learned cascade, raising the bar for a meaningful improvement. None of these sources cover the training-time setting, a policy that co-adapts with and can learn to exploit the proxy over the course of RL, rather than a fixed judge scoring a static model's outputs, so the "learns during training where the proxy is exploitable" half of the claim is not directly evidenced or refuted here; that is a gap this angle cannot close alone.

## 4. Closest prior work from this angle, and how it differs

Trust or Escalate (Jung, Brahman, Choi; arXiv 2407.18370) is the closest prior work in this angle. It already frames verification as a budgeted choice between a cheap proxy and an expensive judge, and already calibrates a confidence-based escalation rule with a provable accuracy guarantee, structurally close to the AVRL allocator. It differs in three ways: (1) it operates at evaluation time on a fixed, already-trained model's outputs, a judging pipeline, not a component inside an RL training loop, so there is no policy co-evolving with the allocator's decisions; (2) its escalation rule is calibrated once against a fixed target, not learned online against an evolving reward-hacking surface, so it cannot exploit prompt-space structure that emerges only during training; (3) it optimizes for human-agreement guarantees, not the RLVR-specific outcomes AVRL cares about, corrupted-reward rate during training and downstream policy quality at equal expensive-call budget. Heteroskedastic Signals (arXiv 2606.15841) is the second-closest: explicitly about budgeted verification cost allocation and structural heterogeneity, but still evaluation-time and static/gated rather than a trained, adaptive, in-the-loop allocator.
