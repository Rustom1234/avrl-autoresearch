# S12 - Adaptive Compute Allocation (test-time scaling, cascades, early exit, speculative/selective verification)

## 1. What this angle contributes to the thesis

This literature answers a narrower question than AVRL asks: given a fixed compute or dollar budget, how should a system decide, per-instance, whether to spend more compute or trust a cheap proxy, and the consistent finding across 2023-2026 work is that instance-adaptive allocation beats uniform allocation by large margins (FrugalGPT's cost reduction, Adaptive-Consistency's sample-budget reduction, Speculative Rejection's Best-of-N speedup). None of this work, however, treats the allocation policy as something that must itself be trained online against a reward signal that the policy's own choices corrupt: cascades and routers are fit once (often offline, on held-out preference or cost data) and then frozen, so they cannot learn mid-training where a proxy becomes exploitable, which is the gap AVRL's in-training allocator targets. The strongest transferable result is architectural, not just empirical: nearly every system in this space (FrugalGPT, RouteLLM, speculative decoding, CALM) is literally a small learned or scored gate sitting in front of an expensive call, which is the same shape as AVRL's allocator deciding real-verifier vs. proxy per rollout group, and their per-query savings numbers (often 2x-98x) set the bar AVRL's in-training version would need to clear or explain away.

## 3. Supports vs. threatens the thesis claim

**Supports:**
- Every cascade/router result above shows the same structural win AVRL claims: a cheap-first, learned, instance-adaptive gate beats spending the expensive resource uniformly, and the margins are large (2x to ~98x), which makes it plausible that an in-training verification allocator has real headroom over a fixed threshold rather than marginal gains (sources 1, 9, 12, 13).
- Adaptive-Consistency (source 12) and Large Language Monkeys (source 3) both show that "cheap proxy agreement" (majority vote / self-consistency) is informative enough to gate on, which is exactly the proxy AVRL proposes trusting when cheap, and both quantify the savings from doing so rather than asserting it.
- CALM (source 11) is direct precedent that a *learned* gate, trained end-to-end rather than hand-tuned, outperforms static thresholds for a compute/quality tradeoff, supporting AVRL's bet that a learned allocator beats a fixed threshold.

**Threatens:**
- Snell et al. (source 2) and Wu et al. (source 5) both suggest that most of the benefit of adaptive allocation comes from a fairly simple difficulty signal (problem hardness, self-consistency spread), which is close to what "Heteroskedastic Signals" (arXiv 2606.15841, per thesis brief) already argues suffices as a static cost-stratified threshold; if instance difficulty alone captures most of the gain, a learned in-training allocator may only be recovering what a good static heuristic already gets, weakening the "learned beats fixed" claim.
- None of sources 1-13 operate under adversarial pressure from a policy that is simultaneously being optimized against the very proxy the allocator is learning to trust; RLHF/RLVR reward hacking is a different regime than static test-time cascades, so the strong empirical wins here may not transfer, and no source here measures "corrupted reward" as an outcome, only cost or accuracy, which is a gap AVRL would need to fill itself rather than borrow.
- Speculative decoding and speculative rejection (sources 9, 10, 13) are exact or near-exact accept/reject schemes with tight statistical guarantees; AVRL's proxy (majority vote, small reward model) has no such guarantee, so the large multiplier numbers from this angle may not be a fair analogy for expected AVRL gains.

## 4. Closest prior work from this angle, and how it differs

The closest single work from this angle is FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia, Zou, 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176): a learned cascade that decides, per query, whether a cheap model's answer is good enough or whether to escalate to a more expensive one, trained on labeled data to predict when escalation is worth it. Structurally this is the nearest analogue to AVRL's allocator: both are learned, per-instance gates between a cheap and an expensive resource, optimizing a cost/quality tradeoff rather than using a fixed rule.

It differs from AVRL in four ways. First, FrugalGPT's cascade is trained offline, once, on a static labeled dataset, then frozen at deployment; AVRL's allocator must adapt online, during RL training, as the policy and the exploitability of the proxy both shift together. Second, FrugalGPT escalates per single query/answer; AVRL allocates per rollout group, a different unit with its own aggregation structure (e.g., variance across a group's rewards). Third, FrugalGPT has no adversarial dynamic: the LLM being queried is not being trained to exploit the cascade's blind spots, whereas in AVRL the policy is optimized against whatever reward the allocator's proxy produces, so gaming is expected, not incidental. Fourth, FrugalGPT reports cost and accuracy, not "corrupted reward" or hacking rate, so it offers no evidence on AVRL's central claim that a learned allocator admits fewer corrupted rewards than a fixed threshold at matched expensive-call budget, that claim is untested by this literature and remains AVRL's to establish.
