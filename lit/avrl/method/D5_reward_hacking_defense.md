# D5: Budgeted verification as a reward-hacking defense

## The idea, in plain language

Right now the project treats budgeted verification as a cost problem: spend the expensive grader where it buys the most accuracy, or catches the most wrong rewards, per dollar. This direction reframes it as a safety problem. Reward hacking is not a fixed property of a task, it is something a policy discovers partway through training. Its signature is a growing gap between what the cheap grader says and what the expensive grader says on the same rollouts, and that gap should appear first in a specific region of prompt space, not everywhere at once (the confident-subset clustering already measured on the open-ended task, E09d, neighbor ratio 2.02 inside the top-quartile reward-model-gap cell). So: keep a small, fixed, never-selected-by-the-policy "canary" sample, fully verify it on a schedule, watch the cheap-versus-expensive gap per prompt cluster over training steps, and when a cluster's gap spikes, pull expensive-verification budget from quiet clusters into that one. The alarm and the defense are the same mechanism: divergence is both the thing measured and the trigger that reallocates spend.

## The mechanism, precisely

1. At initialization, hold out a canary set of prompt groups (order 500 to 1,000), stratified by the embedding clusters already computed in phase 0 (`p0_analyze.py`'s cell assignment). The canary set is never chosen by any allocator; it is always fully verified on a fixed schedule (every K steps), so its labels cannot be biased by the policy's own selection, the specific failure the project's ledger flagged for the learned allocator (seed-0 note: "the online allocator learns from the biased sample of groups it chose to verify while the policy drifts underneath it").
2. For each cluster c, compute divergence(c, t) = mean(cheap_reward - expensive_reward) on the canary groups in c at checkpoint t, and a per-cluster noise band from the first two checkpoints.
3. An alarm for c fires when divergence(c, t) exceeds its baseline by more than a fixed multiple of its noise band (e.g., 2x). This is the direct, during-training hacking measurement: a trajectory, not one post-hoc accuracy comparison.
4. When the alarm fires, the main allocator (otherwise a static budget-matched threshold) reallocates verification budget from the quietest clusters to c for the next window, holding total expensive calls fixed. No policy is trained; the rule is verify by divergence rank, within budget, refreshed each window.
5. Hacking is scored directly with an injected, ground-truth-labeled exploit (see below), not inferred solely from the divergence curve, so the alarm's claims can be checked against a known-true hack rate.

## Prior art, what each already did, and where this differs

- Baker et al. (2025), OpenAI, arXiv 2503.11926, https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926. They put a fixed-rate chain-of-thought monitor into RL training and penalized visible hacking; under continued pressure the policy learned to hide the hack instead of stopping ("monitorability tax"). Their monitor is a constant-rate classifier on a separate signal (CoT text), applied as a penalty every step, not a budget-constrained comparison between two reward signals the loop already computes that reallocates a scarce resource toward rising divergence. This alarm reuses signals already present (cheap, expensive reward) and spends adaptively rather than taxing everywhere.
- Shao et al. (2025/2026), "Spurious Rewards," arXiv 2506.10947, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10947. RLVR with a random or degenerate reward signal still produced large accuracy gains on some Qwen math models, establishing proxy-signal exploitation is real and large. This is a single end-of-training comparison, not a trajectory, and it diagnoses but does not detect or correct. It motivates treating divergence as a live signal, not a once-at-the-end check.
- Gao, Schulman, Hilton (2022), arXiv 2210.10760, https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760. Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization: the true-versus-proxy reward gap grows as a predictable function of optimization pressure, supporting the idea that a divergence trajectory is trackable and lawful, not noise, which the alarm's noise-band threshold relies on.
- Jung, Brahman, Choi (ICLR 2025), arXiv 2407.18370, https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370. Trust or Escalate is a fixed cascade with a confidence-calibrated escalation rule, set once, for evaluation-time human-agreement guarantees. It never revisits the rule as a policy drifts during training, exactly the gap this direction targets.
- This project's own ledger (`avrl/STATUS.md`, entry P1-goldberg-s0): the self-training learned allocator admitted more corrupted rewards in-training than the static threshold (72 vs. 27 at equal budget), and the gap widened over training. Any new mechanism must explain, and avoid, that failure, not just claim to beat the threshold again.

## The exact novelty claim that survives

Not "a learned allocator beats a fixed threshold," already contradicted by the project's own in-training result on the self-consistency task. The narrower claim: a divergence alarm from an unbiased, periodically-verified canary sample, used only to reallocate (not learn a policy over) a fixed budget, can suppress a directly measured, injected exploit faster and to a lower plateau than a static budget-matched threshold, where the cheap signal is weak and errors cluster (open-ended, AUC 0.75-0.80), without the learned allocator's self-selection-bias failure, because the canary sample is never chosen by what it scores.

## Smallest experiment

Inject a known exploit into the GSM8K harness so hacking is ground-truth labeled, not inferred. In `avrl/p1_grpo.py`, add a flag that adds a small, fixed bonus to the cheap (majority-vote) reward whenever a completion contains a specific marker string, with zero effect on the expensive (gold-answer) reward; log `hack_rate` per step as the fraction of completions containing the marker (the string match is nearly free, no new infra). In `avrl/allocators.py`, add a `DivergenceAllocator` that holds a fixed canary subset of groups (drawn once, excluded from all arms' trainable selection), reads `p0_analyze.py`'s existing cluster assignments, and reallocates budget by the rule above. Run three budget-matched arms at 25 percent (existing `ThresholdAllocator`, existing `LearnedAllocator`, new `DivergenceAllocator`) for 150-200 steps, 2 seeds, same as the existing phase-1 protocol. Compare `hack_rate` trajectories and corrupted-reward admissions across arms.

## Publication shape

A workshop paper rather than a main-track submission (the result is one mechanism tested on two task families, not a new training algorithm): target a 2026/2027 reliable-ML or LLM-safety workshop (e.g., a NeurIPS or ICLR workshop on monitoring, oversight, or reward design). ## Product angle

A drop-in wrapper for TRL GRPO: supply the existing cheap reward function and an expensive grader (a judge call or ground-truth check) plus a call budget, and get a callback, `HackAlarm(cheap_fn, expensive_fn, budget=0.25)`, that replaces the `reward_funcs` entry. What a user would see: a per-cluster divergence log (console or W&B) and one "hacking risk" number per prompt region, rising before held-out accuracy shows anything wrong, with the expensive budget automatically following the alarm instead of being spent uniformly. The pitch: train a small model with a cheap grader and a budget-capped expensive one, and get told when and where it starts gaming the cheap grader, before the rest of the expensive budget is spent finding out the slow way.
