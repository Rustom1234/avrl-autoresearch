# Where to spend the expensive check: budgeted verification in RL training and in the research loop that studied it

Rustom Dubash, Haverford College. Work from 24 September to 19 October 2026. Status: pre-registered results, one live follow-up pending human adjudication.

## 1. For a reader with no technical background

Modern language models are improved by a loop: the model attempts thousands of tasks, each attempt is graded, and the model is nudged toward what graded well. Grading is the expensive part. A cheap grader (a small model, or a rule such as "did most attempts agree?") can score everything but is sometimes wrong. An expensive grader (a large model, a full test suite, a human) is right more often but cannot be afforded for every attempt. The thesis asks a budgeting question: given a fixed budget of expensive checks, which attempts should get them?

The proposed answer was a learned router, a small program that learns, as training runs, where the cheap grader tends to be wrong and sends those cases to the expensive grader. Three claims were written down before any experiment and each had a kill condition.

What the experiments found, stated plainly:

- A very simple rule does the job. Send the expensive grader the attempts where the cheap grader is least sure of itself (for instance, where the model's own attempts disagree with each other). With that rule, spending the expensive grader on one in four attempts produced models as good as spending it on all of them. This held on math, on code and on free-text writing, at small scale and at a 7-billion-parameter scale with two seeds.
- The learned router never beat that simple rule at the same budget. Where the cheap grader's confidence signal was strong the learned router lost; where it was weak the two tied.
- The idea that the router could learn "where" in the space of prompts the cheap grader fails was tested seven ways and failed every time.
- The cheap grader's error rate, measured before training starts, predicted how training would go. A short burn-in (grade everything for a few minutes, measure the cheap grader against the expensive one, then set the budget) turned that into a tool.
- One warning sign appeared only at scale and under high optimization pressure: the model learned to please the cheap grader while a second, independent judge saw it getting worse. Two of three independent judges agreed on the decline, one did not, so it is reported as a mixed result.

The second half of the project is unusual. The experiments above were not run by hand. A small autonomous loop of four AI agents (one plans the next experiment, one runs it, one writes it up with a verdict against the pre-registered thresholds, one checks the numbers and corrects the write-up) ran the campaign and kept an append-only ledger, now 120 entries long, of what was tried, what was found and what was corrected. The project then turned the same budgeting question on the loop itself: when should a cheap model's work be checked by an expensive one, and does it matter which model does which job? Those four follow-up experiments are the part most relevant to anyone building AI systems that run research or engineering work autonomously.

Why this is hiring-relevant: every result above, positive or negative, was pre-registered with a pass and a kill condition before the run, checked by an independent critic, and reported with its failures intact. The work shows the ability to frame a question so it can lose, to build the tooling that lets a machine run the campaign honestly, and to measure the machine afterwards.

## 2. For a reader with basic technical knowledge

**Setting.** GRPO-style RL post-training with LoRA adapters on Qwen2.5 policies (0.5B to 7B). The cheap proxy reward was majority vote over sampled answers (GSM8K), agreement among programs passing a visible test (MBPP), or a 0.6B reward model (HelpSteer2 free text). The expensive verifier was the gold answer, the full test suite, or a 3B to 14B judge. Per rollout group, an allocator decided whether to pay the verifier. Logged per step: expensive calls, corrupted rewards admitted (gold used for logging only), and held-out quality on a fixed evaluation subset.

**Pre-registered claims and kill tests.** Claim 1: a quarter of the verifier calls gives the same held-out quality as full verification. Claim 2: a learned allocator admits fewer corrupted rewards than any fixed rule at equal budget. Claim 3: the edge comes from structure in prompt-embedding space (kill if embeddings add under 0.05 AUC to cheap features and the neighbor ratio of proxy error is under 1.5).

**Phase 0, structure.** Nine task and proxy pairs, 600 to 4,000 groups each. Cheap-feature AUC for predicting proxy error ranged 0.73 to 0.93; embedding gain never exceeded 0.04; neighbor ratio about 1.0. Claim 3 killed on every pair.

**Phase 1, in training (about 60 runs, 100 to 600 steps, two to four seeds).** A label-free threshold on the cheap grader's own confidence was never beaten at equal budget. Example, GSM8K at a 25 percent budget: threshold 26 corrupted groups per seed against 62 to 63 for the cell bandit and 33 to 35 for the greedy self-trainer. In the weak-signal free-text regime the best learned allocator tied the threshold over nine comparisons (pooled 397 vs 452 corrupted groups, short of the pre-registered bar). Held-out quality never separated by allocation rule, from zero verification to full, so the budget changed how many wrong rewards the policy saw, not how good the policy became. No cheap grader drifted or was gamed over the horizons tested. The burn-in diagnostic (verify everything for 25 steps, run the regime statistic, pick the budget) was validated on real runs.

**S1, scale (Qwen2.5-7B policy, Skywork 0.6B reward model, Qwen2.5-14B judge, 4,000 steps per arm, four A100s).** Claim 1 passed: the threshold at 25 percent of judge calls scored 7.725 and 7.76 against full verification's 7.645 and 7.67 (bar 0.15, untrained 7.455). The full-verification arm trains on an absolute score reward with no gradient on about 57 percent of groups, so a rank-shaped full arm (the fair bound) is still owed. Overoptimization appeared only in the proxy-only arm at three times the learning rate: Qwen-judge score peaked at 7.70 and fell to 7.48 while the cheap score rose to 10.6; Phi-4 agreed, OLMo-2 13B reversed; responses grew 14 percent longer. Reported as judge-dependent, one seed.

**The loop.** A 46-line orchestrator runs planner, runner, analyst and critic agents per iteration, each with a JSON schema. It produced about 40 ledger entries in four days with critic corrections to narrative numbers in several. Its failure mode was drift toward cheap variants of the same analysis. A 6-token evaluation bug was caught by a cross-run re-evaluation, not by the critic.

**AR1, planner context (offline replay, 3 arms x 5 seeds).** With the 52 critic-checked entries as an oracle, a planner seeing full logs reached the correct claim verdicts in 5.2 experiments on average, a compressed ledger in 7.2, compressed plus "inspiration" dead ends in 10.2. Both pre-registered predictions failed; the recommendation reversed to "give the planner the whole ledger while it fits".

**AR2 and AR3, the critic as a cascade.** AR2: Haiku and Sonnet locate planted defects at similar rates, but Haiku flags half of untouched entries and Sonnet one in five, at twelve times the cost. AR3 is the thesis's claim 1 rebuilt on the critic: three Haiku passes, agreement among them as the confidence signal, the lowest-agreement quarter escalated to Sonnet, burn-in sets the budget. 64 entries, 328 items with five planted-defect kinds, 2,624 calls, blind adjudication of flagged controls. Pre-registered KILL: Haiku's recall on plants is at least Sonnet's (0.91 to 1.00 vs 0.82 to 1.00), but it flags 79 percent of clean entries against Sonnet's 31 percent, and it does so unanimously (86 percent of fully agreeing Haiku verdicts on clean entries are wrong), so agreement is uninformative (AUROC 0.58) and the cascade equals random escalation. The burn-in diagnostic predicted this before test rows were opened (recommended budget 0.75, signal labelled uninformative). The signal that works on the critic is the one that was useless in RL, verbalized confidence (AUROC 0.86). Mechanism: consistency works when the cheap grader's errors are stochastic and fails when they are systematic. Side finding: 36 percent of ledger entries carried a real internal contradiction the file-based critic had missed.

**AR5, the live routed loop (five copies, 7 h 15 min each, one per GPU host, identical leak-gated seed).** Arms: all Sonnet twice; Haiku planner, runner and analyst with a Sonnet critic; all Haiku; Haiku roles with a screened Sonnet critic. Graded by a machine gate on real result files plus a blind three-pass adjudicator against the original campaign's verdicts; every entry audited by a Sonnet auditor; cost from transcripts. 466 agents, 85 experiments. The pre-registered noise gate fired: the two all-Sonnet copies disagreed (one settled on the right verdicts after 11 experiments and 40 dollars; the other declared claim 2 supported from its third experiment and held it). So no routing contrast is claimed. Descriptively: the screened-critic arm settled fastest (5 experiments, 82 minutes, 2.6 dollars) because its planner left the seed rollouts for a second proxy early; the two Haiku arms that never left the seed data could not kill claim 3 in 21 and 16 experiments; the screeners flagged every entry, so the cascade never saved a strong-critic call; material errors in critic-corrected text were rare in every arm including all-Haiku (0 to 1 per arm). Haiku roles cost a third to a twentieth of all-Sonnet. Reading: in a live loop the high-leverage variable is one planner decision per iteration, which no verifier repairs, and run-to-run variance of a single model exceeds the model-routing effects at n = 1.

## 3. Status, limits and what is next

Established: claim 1 at small and 7B scale; the negative result on learned allocators; the regime diagnostic and burn-in tool; the AR1 and AR3 negatives with mechanisms; the AR5 variance measurement. Open: the rank-shaped full arm at 7B (the fair claim-1 bound), a second seed and a length-controlled judge comparison for the overoptimization result, Rustom's adjudication of the dissenting all-Sonnet run's claim-2 entries, and more all-Sonnet replicates to measure the loop's variance floor before any routing contrast is attempted. Limits stated throughout: one project and one campaign as the substrate for the loop experiments; Sonnet as auditor and adjudicator, validated on planted and seeded defects only; n = 1 per Haiku arm; the live loop's agents received the session's global instructions, identically across arms.

Artifacts: avrl/RESEARCH_LOG.md (the ledger, 120 entries), avrl/PROJECT.md (claims and standing), avrl/exp/ (every experiment script, including the AR1 to AR5 harnesses), avrl/runs/ (results), lit/avrl/ (literature sweeps, synthesis, proposal, figures).
