# AVRL status, 2026-10-01

## The task

The thesis question, chosen at the advisor meeting on Sept 22, is whether a learned allocator that decides when to pay for an expensive verifier during RL post-training of a language model beats a fixed rule. The inspiration is SOFisher, a microscopy paper where a policy chooses which patch of a slide to image next under a budget. Three claims were pre-registered: (1) equal policy quality at a fraction of expensive verifier calls, (2) fewer corrupted rewards admitted into training than any fixed rule at the same budget, (3) the edge comes from structure in prompt space.

On Sept 30 the brief was: run a large literature review, then set up an autonomous research loop that keeps running experiments on goldberg and hannah and makes the project thesis-ready.

## What was built

Literature. One workflow with 34 agents: 14 search angles in parallel (RL post-training cost, judge cascades, reward-model error, reward hacking, proxy-reward RL, budgeted active search theory, prompt selection in RLVR, verifiers, error structure, frameworks, SOFisher framing, adaptive compute, tasks and models, 2026 work), a citation-existence check on every file (no fabricated sources found), a synthesis with a checkable gap statement, a thesis proposal, a 20-experiment pre-registered backlog, and three adversarial gap checks. The gap checks changed the framing: the novelty is a new combination plus a corrupted-reward metric rather than a new mechanism (VIP already learns in-training budget allocation for rollout counts), the primary baseline must be a periodically recalibrated threshold rather than a fixed-once rule, and what makes it a thesis is a predictive statistic for when proxy error clusters, tested by transfer across tasks. The proposal was revised to say exactly that.

The autoresearch loop (avrl/autoresearch.workflow.js). Each iteration runs four agents in sequence. A planner reads the backlog, the ledger and the newest results and picks the experiment with the highest information value per GPU hour that fits a 45-minute cap, preferring experiments that can kill a claim. A runner implements it by copying and modifying the existing scripts, launches it detached with a done flag, polls, and caps it at 45 minutes. An analyst reads the raw result files, appends a ledger entry (hypothesis, run, a table of numbers, verdict against the pre-registered pass and kill thresholds, what it changes, follow-ups), and appends the follow-ups to the backlog. A critic re-derives every number from the raw files, checks leakage, budget parity and seed adequacy, and appends a correction note if anything is wrong. The loop stops after a fixed number of iterations or when the planner finds nothing runnable. Guardrails: one GPU job at a time, all writes under one directory, no git pushes, kill patterns that cannot hit the agent's own shell. In practice the loop ran eight iterations plus one direct pass, and the critic corrected narrative numbers in three entries without overturning any verdict. Its one failure mode was drift toward cheap variants of the same replay; a priority note in the backlog redirected it, and the one experiment it still skipped (the code task) was run under an explicit spec.

Harnesses. Phase 0 samples rollouts from the policy, defines a cheap proxy reward and an expensive verifier, and measures three structure statistics (cheap-feature AUC for proxy error, embedding neighbor ratio, confident-subset error rate) plus a budgeted allocation replay with nine policies and a bootstrap. Phase 1 is GRPO with LoRA and a pluggable verification allocator, with per-step logging of expensive calls and corrupted rewards and periodic held-out evaluation, in two flavours: exact-answer tasks (GSM8K) and judge-scored open-ended tasks (HelpSteer2 prompts, a 0.6B reward model as the cheap proxy, a 3B or 7B judge as the verifier). Infrastructure found along the way: goldberg's GPU is a 16 GB A4000, not the 32 GB card in the notes; Triton needs Python headers exported via CPATH; a 7B judge only fits this card quantized or in eager mode; the training learning rate of 1e-6 was too small to move a LoRA policy.

## What to tell the advisor

The SOFisher transfer became a measured answer rather than a method: a learned verifier allocator beats a fixed threshold only when the cheap confidence signal is weak, and a pre-registered statistic predicts which regime a task is in. The evidence is nine replay points with bootstrap intervals and four in-training runs, all pre-registered and independently re-checked. The next result, the in-training comparison on the weak-signal task at a working learning rate, decides whether the thesis is a method paper or a measurement paper.

## 2026-10-02 12:30 UTC: hannah running, the OLMo question answered twice, and the product's first piece built

**The question, restated.** During reinforcement learning post-training, a cheap grader (majority vote, a small reward model, a visible test case) scores every rollout group and an expensive grader (gold answer, full test suite, a large judge) is only affordable for a fraction of them. Which groups should get the expensive call? The thesis tests whether a learned allocator beats a fixed rule, and what the fixed rule should be.

**Where the claims stand (six in-training settings, two seeds where run).**
- Claim 1, equal quality at a quarter of the verifier calls: PASS with the exact bound now measured. On GSM8K with the 1.5B policy, verifying every group (800 expensive calls) ends at 0.663 accuracy on both seeds; the vote-agreement threshold at 200 calls ends at 0.690 and 0.667. Every arm, including the bandit that admitted 62 corrupted groups, is within the 300-prompt eval's noise.
- Claim 2, a learned allocator admits fewer corrupted rewards than any fixed rule: KILLED in all six settings. With the 7B judge on the open-ended task (M09b, two seeds) every rule ties: consistency threshold 64 and 54, reward-model-gap rule 64, greedy self-trainer 58 and 53, bandit 59.
- Claim 3, prompt-space structure helps: KILLED; the cell bandit is worst or tied-worst everywhere.
- The narrowed statement: a threshold on the cheap grader's own confidence is never beaten at equal budget; its gain over random verification scales with the cheap grader's base error and the confidence AUC (large on GSM8K and the 3B-judge task, nil with the 7B judge where base error is 6 percent).

**Why corrupted-reward counts matter sometimes and not others.** Today's two reference experiments bracket it. On GSM8K, 4 to 10 percent corrupted groups did not move accuracy at all (M10). **How the code works, for the pieces that changed today.**
- The regime diagnostic (`avrl/avrl_router/diagnose.py`): takes a table with one row per rollout group, a confidence column (higher means the cheap grader is surer) and an error column (1 when the cheap grader disagreed with the expensive one). It sorts groups by confidence, pretends to verify the least confident fraction at each budget, and counts how many wrong rewards would slip through among the rest, against the random-allocation expectation. Bootstraps over groups give intervals. It also reports the AUC (how well low confidence predicts error) and the smallest budget that keeps corruption under a target. Validated on seven saved rollout sets; on the open-ended task it reproduces the training result from rollouts alone (consistency usable, reward-model margin uninformative).
- Per-group logging (H1): every harness now writes one line per group per step with its confidence features, whether it was verified, and whether its cheap reward was wrong. This is the data the diagnostic needs during training and what lets the in-training AUC be measured, which was impossible from the per-step totals.
- The visible-test grader (`--proxy visible` in `avrl/p1_code_grpo.py`): each program is run twice in the sandbox, once against the first assert only (cheap reward) and once against all asserts (expensive reward, used for verified groups and for logging). A group counts as corrupted when some program passes the visible assert but fails the hidden ones. The threshold arm's confidence is the agreement of output signatures among the programs that passed the visible test.
- The curriculum (`--curriculum ids.json`): restricts the training prompt pool to listed task ids before the seeded shuffle, so arms still see identical prompt order.

## 2026-10-05 21:15 UTC: the scale-up (S1) is running on hannah

**Why.** The project's conclusions came from policies of 0.5 to 1.5 billion parameters, judges of 3 to 7 billion, and runs of 100 to 600 steps. Two of them are exposed to that: "the verification budget does not change held-out quality" and "no cheap grader was gamed". The reward-model overoptimization literature predicts that a larger policy trained longer against a small reward model raises its proxy reward while its true quality falls, which these runs were too small and short to show.

**What is running.** A Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct policy (LoRA, gradient checkpointing, pinned to one A100) with the Skywork 0.6B reward model as the cheap grader and a Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct judge on a second A100 as the expensive grader, on the free-text task, 2000 steps per arm (the same number of groups as 1000 steps at the small-scale batch). Three chained jobs: proxy-only and consistency threshold (seed 0); full verification and a second proxy-only arm at three times the learning rate (the drift probe under more pressure); consistency threshold and full at seed 1. Each job takes about 27 hours; the chain ends around October 9. Pre-registered tests: drift (corruption rate in the last 200 steps against the first 200), overoptimization (held-out judge score falling from its peak while the raw reward-model score keeps rising, which the harness now logs), and claim 1 at scale (threshold within 0.15 of full on two seeds).

**What it took to fit.** Four probes: a 7B policy at the small-scale batch does not fit one 40 GB card (the first probe passed only because the trainer had spread it over two cards); with two visible cards the trainer also tries to replicate the policy onto the judge's card; the 14B judge needs chunked scoring; and the training forward's full-vocabulary logits needed a smaller micro-batch to leave headroom (final peak 36.9 of 40 GB at 47 seconds per step). Three adversarial design reviews ran before submission and their fixes are in the pre-registration (an unbounded proxy signal, minimum windows for the drift test, a second seed for claim 1, and the stated caveats: in-family judge, the full arm training on its own evaluator, and the rank-versus-absolute reward shape).

**First readings (October 6, 00:45 UTC, job a at about step 300 of 2000).** The untrained 7B model scores 7.455 on the fixed 200-prompt evaluation under the 14B judge, and both arms scored the same at step 200, so nothing has moved yet. In the first 200 steps the cheap reward model disagreed with the 14B judge on 14 percent of groups, the highest base error of any working grader in the project, and the consistency threshold removed little of it (12 percent against 14 for random allocation), which the weak free-text confidence signal predicted. Evaluations land every 200 steps; the drift and overoptimization verdicts need at least 600 steps and the full run respectively.

**Two restarts (October 6).** The first attempt ran out of GPU memory at step 338 in both arms on a long prompt batch during generation, so the reward model moved to the judge's card and the generation batch halved to 2 prompts per step with the step count doubled to 4000. The second attempt then exposed a rounding gap: at 2 groups per step the 25 percent budget rounded to zero verified groups, so the threshold arm was not verifying anything; the budget rule now uses cumulative rounding (one verified group on alternate steps, exactly 25 percent over the run), which leaves every earlier result unchanged. The third attempt is running; at 2 groups per step the threshold rule only chooses the less confident of two, so its corruption reduction at scale is a lower bound on the small-batch rule.
